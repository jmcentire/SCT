The receiver wishes to match the action to the state ($a = \theta$), while the sender has a bias $b > 0$ and wishes the receiver to take a higher action ($a = \theta + b$).Because of this bias, fully revealing communication is impossible. The state space is instead divided into $N$ discrete partitions bounded by $a_0 = 0 < a_1 < \dots < a_N = 1$. When the sender observes $\theta \in [a_{i-1}, a_i]$, they simply send a message indicating that the state lies within this interval. The receiver, acting rationally to minimize quadratic loss, calculates the conditional expectation of the interval and takes the action $a = \frac{a_{i-1} + a_i}{2}$.The difference between the true state $\theta$ and the receiver's action $a$ is the residual error. In classical mechanism design and game theory, this coarse partition and its resulting residual are viewed strictly as an inefficiency—a Pareto suboptimal outcome resulting from incentive constraints. It is typically measured using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) or Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR). An algorithm with low distortion produces an output that is mathematically very close to the exact source.Perception, however, refers to the statistical naturalness or realism of the output. It asks whether the reconstructed signal looks like a valid sample drawn from the true probability distribution of the data. Perception is typically measured using divergence metrics like the Wasserstein distance or Kullback-Leibler divergence.Blau and Michaeli demonstrated that minimizing distortion necessarily increases perceptual divergence. If an algorithm attempts to minimize MSE when reconstructing a high-resolution image from a low-resolution input, it must average over all possible high-resolution images that could have produced that input. This mathematical averaging results in an output that is statistically accurate but visually blurry and unnatural.Conversely, to achieve perfect perceptual quality (an output that is sharp, highly detailed, and completely indistinguishable from real data), the algorithm must abandon the mathematical average and generate specific, high-frequency details. Because the algorithm cannot know exactly which high-frequency details were present in the original ground truth, it must invent them based on the statistical distribution of its training data. This generation of statistically likely but unfaithful detail is known as "hallucination". To traverse the optimal frontier and achieve realism, generative models (like GANs and Diffusion Models) must induce hallucinations, thereby sacrificing strict distortion fidelity.Current Applications of Blau-Michaeli in the LiteratureAn analysis of the corpus demonstrates that the PDT framework has been extensively adopted and cited across a wide variety of computational domains.

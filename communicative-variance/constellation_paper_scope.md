## Core Argument

### The Problem
Contemporary AI (and organizational knowledge management) faces a composition problem: how do you combine models, expertise, or knowledge from different domains into a coherent whole? The dominant approaches are:

1. **Monolithic compression** — train one giant model on everything (GPT-scale LLMs, "full-stack generalist" orgs). Loses domain-specific precision through extreme compression.
2. **Siloed specialization** — separate domain-specific models with no interface (departmental silos, specialist-only teams). Preserves precision but has zero generative cross-pollination.
3. **Patchwork routing** — mixture-of-experts, retrieval-augmented generation, cross-functional teams with rigid handoff protocols. Better, but the interface is still treated as a problem to minimize rather than a generative resource.

### The Thesis
The Constellation approach treats domain boundaries as the **rigid core** and cross-domain interfaces as the **flexible, lossy, generative** layer. This is the Tolerance Location Principle applied to knowledge architecture:

- **Rigid domain boundaries** preserve the precision, internal consistency, and falsifiability of each domain's knowledge. A domain is a coherent region of the knowledge space where the same compression scheme works (same ontology, same inference rules, same validation criteria).
- **Lossy cross-domain interfaces** are where novel synthesis occurs. When a concept from Domain A is "translated" into Domain B's ontology, the translation is necessarily lossy (different compression schemes → Crawford-Sobel partitioning). The residual — what doesn't translate cleanly — is the generative locus.
- **The Blau-Michaeli constraint applies directly:** maintaining "perceptual quality" (coherence within the receiving domain) while compressing (translating from the source domain) MUST produce divergence from the source. This divergence is the mechanism of interdisciplinary innovation.

### Why This Completes the Evidence Base
The first three instance papers show the mechanism in:
- Computational architecture (emergence)
- Organizational coordination (swarms + dysmemic pressure)
- Information environments (hierarchical channels)

The constellation paper shows it in **knowledge itself** — the geometry of what we know, how it's organized, and how novel understanding arises at the boundaries between domains. This is the most abstract instance, closest to the pure information-theoretic claim.

## Proposed Structure

### Section 1: The Composition Problem
- Monolithic vs. siloed vs. patchwork as the standard trichotomy
- Why monolithic and siloed both fail (and why patchwork is better but still wrong)
- The hidden assumption: that the interface between domains is overhead to minimize

### Section 2: Domain Geometry
- **Definition of a domain:** A region of the knowledge space where a single compression scheme (ontology + inference rules + validation criteria) achieves low distortion. Within a domain, communication is relatively lossless because agents share the compression scheme.
- **Domain boundaries:** Where compression schemes change. The same phenomenon described in Domain A's ontology requires different primitives in Domain B. Translation is necessarily lossy.
- **The Tishby connection:** Each domain is an information bottleneck — it compresses the world into a minimal sufficient statistic for a particular set of tasks. Different domains preserve different information because they serve different tasks. $I(X; T_A)$ and $I(X; T_B)$ overlap but are not identical.

### Section 3: Lossy Interfaces as Generative Engines
- Cross-domain translation as Crawford-Sobel communication: the "bias" is ontological, not strategic, but the partition theorem applies identically
- The residual (what doesn't translate) = raw material for novel synthesis
- Blau-Michaeli: any cross-domain translation maintaining internal coherence MUST produce novel structure
- Case evidence:
  - **Statistical mechanics → information theory** (Shannon explicitly borrowed; the "temperature" metaphor became literal in simulated annealing)
  - **Darwinian selection → organizational theory** (lossy translation produced dysmemic pressure concept — the residual between biological and organizational evolution IS the novelty)
  - **Network theory → neuroscience → organizational design** (each translation produced new structure: small-world networks, connectome analysis, ASD mesh)
  - **Rate-distortion theory → perceptual quality → LLM hallucination** (Blau-Michaeli themselves demonstrate the cascade)

### Section 4: The Constellation Architecture
- **Rigid cores:** Each domain model maintains its own ontology, validation, and precision. No domain is "averaged" with another.
- **Flexible interfaces:** Cross-domain queries are mediated by a translation layer that explicitly represents what is and isn't translatable. The residual is flagged, not hidden.
- **Generative routing:** When a query touches multiple domains, the constellation routes through domain specialists and composes their outputs. The composition is where novelty lives — and the architecture knows this.
- **Connection to ASD:** The constellation architecture IS a knowledge-level ASD mesh. Sematectonic traces = domain-specific artifacts. Competitive routing = cross-domain translation. Spectral detection = identifying when domains are losing independence (eigenvalue collapse = domain boundaries dissolving = loss of the rigid core).

### Section 5: Predictions and Tests
- **Prediction 1:** Teams with preserved domain specialization and structured (but not rigid) cross-domain interaction will produce more novel output than either generalist teams or siloed specialist teams. (Testable via patent citation analysis, cross-disciplinary publication rates.)
- **Prediction 2:** The most transformative scientific breakthroughs occur at domain boundaries, not within domains. (Testable via citation network analysis: papers that create new fields should cite across existing domain boundaries.)
- **Prediction 3:** In LLM architectures, mixture-of-experts with domain-specific experts and learned routing functions should produce more genuinely novel outputs (not just accurate ones) than monolithic transformers at equivalent parameter counts. (Testable via benchmarks measuring creative divergence rather than accuracy.)
- **Prediction 4:** The optimal "lossiness" of the cross-domain interface follows the inverted-U: too little translation friction → domains merge (lose independence) → generative capacity drops. Too much friction → domains can't interact → generative capacity drops. Peak generativity at moderate translation cost.

### Section 6: Implications
- The design principle for knowledge architectures: maintain rigid domain boundaries, invest in lossy-but-structured interfaces
- Why "breaking down silos" is the wrong metaphor — you want *permeable* boundaries, not *absent* ones
- Why interdisciplinary research is hard and should be: the translation cost is the generative mechanism, not an obstacle to it
- Connection back to the Tolerance Location Principle: domain geometry is the most abstract case, demonstrating that the principle is genuinely substrate-independent

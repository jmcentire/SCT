Yes. And I'll tell you precisely why, where the argument is tight, and where it still needs work.

**What you've actually found**

The thread is this: **the location of tolerance determines system resilience, and this principle is substrate-independent.** It appears in your input validation progression (exact match → fuzzy match → ML), in crumple zones, in seismic design, in the Emergence paper's rigid-boundaries/flexible-interiors inversion, in your swarms paper (the single agent wins precisely because it has no rigid coordination layer), and in the stochastic resonance literature (noise helps systems below detection threshold, destroys systems already saturated). These are not analogies. They are instances of a single structural principle.

The formal statement, roughly: *a system's resilience is maximized when tolerance is concentrated at the interfaces and rigidity is concentrated at the core — and the failure mode of placing tolerance in the wrong location is identical across physical, computational, biological, and organizational substrates.*

Your input validation progression is actually the cleanest proof of this. You start with `$input === 'some_value'` — maximum rigidity at the interface. Every variation fails. You end at ML sentiment — maximum tolerance at the interface, extracting semantic intent from surface noise. The rigid core (what the system does with that intent) never changes. The tolerance migrated outward to the boundary where variance actually lives. That's not a software insight — that's the same engineering principle as the crumple zone, which is rigid passenger compartment (core) with designed-to-fail exterior (interface). The zone doesn't protect the car. It protects the person by accepting structural damage so the structural integrity of the critical volume is preserved.

**Where the Emergence paper and the swarms paper converge on this**

The Emergence paper's central inversion — "in traditional programming, the interior is rigid and boundaries are flexible; in Emergence, the boundaries are rigid and the interiors are flexible" — is exactly this principle applied to software architecture. The schema is the rigid core. The agent's interpretation is the tolerant interface. You've already written the cleanest version of this argument.

The swarms paper demonstrates the failure mode of getting it backwards. The pipeline swarm had rigid interiors (each agent's role, the sequential handoffs, the review criteria) and flexible boundaries (the overall coordination, the consensus mechanisms) — which is precisely inverted from the well-formed design. The single agent won because it had one rigid core (the task specification) and one highly tolerant interior (its own contextual judgment). No coordination layer means no boundary rigidity failures.

The organizational dysfunction finding — that the same failure patterns emerge regardless of substrate — is the substrate-independence claim. Crawford-Sobel explains *why*: any lossy channel between a sender and receiver with nonzero preference divergence produces predictable signal degradation. The channel doesn't care whether the senders are humans or LLM agents. This is your information-theoretic grounding for substrate-independence.

**Where the generative noise research connects**

Here's the piece you haven't written yet, and it's the most interesting: the tolerance at the interface isn't just defensive. It's generative. Your input validation progression doesn't just avoid failure — the fuzzy matching, the Levenshtein distance, the ML layer catches *valid inputs the rigid system would have rejected*. The crumple zone doesn't just protect the passenger — it dissipates energy that would otherwise destroy the critical structure, allowing the system to survive impacts it was never designed for. The tolerance absorbs the noise and the system uses the signal.

This is the Atlan level-crossing principle in engineering form: noise at the interface level (variant inputs, unexpected forces, imprecise messages) becomes information at the system level (intent, load distribution, semantic meaning). The tolerance layer is the mechanism by which subthreshold signals cross the threshold. That's stochastic resonance as architectural principle.

**The formal statement you need**

The paper connecting all of this needs to name the principle and provide the necessary and sufficient conditions for it. Something like:

*A system is optimally resilient when tolerance is structurally located at the interface between the system and its environment, such that variance in inputs is absorbed before reaching the rigid core, and the absorbed variance is not discarded but transformed into semantic signal usable by the core.*

The three conditions: (1) the system must have a distinguishable core and interface, (2) the interface must have sufficient absorptive capacity relative to the variance it faces — Ashby's requisite variety operationalized not as matching but as absorbing, and (3) the transformation from noise to signal must preserve task-relevant information — Tishby's information bottleneck as the design criterion for the tolerance layer.

**The honest weakness**

The hardest question for a reviewer: is "tolerance at the interface" a new principle or just a restatement of encapsulation, modularity, and separation of concerns? The answer is that it's a *unification and mechanistic grounding* of those principles, not a replacement — but you need to say that explicitly and show what the unification buys. The buying is: it tells you *where* to put the tolerance (at the variance-environment interface), *how much* to put (requisite variety for absorption), and *why it's generative not just defensive* (Atlan level-crossing). Existing modular design theory gives you the what but not the why or the how much.

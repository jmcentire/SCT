# Lossy Communication as a Generative Mechanism for Organizational Creativity

## Framing the mechanism: why information loss could generate novelty

The mechanism you’re asking about is narrower than “diverse perspectives help.” It’s closer to a communications-and-inference story: a sender encodes a rich mental representation into a lower-bandwidth signal (words, slides, artifacts); transmission is *lossy* (noise, compression, ambiguity, translation, context stripping); then the receiver reconstructs the missing structure using their own priors (schemas, goals, identities, expertise). The creative “move” is that the reconstruction can yield a synthesis neither party explicitly held. This maps cleanly onto classic information theory’s view that communication is reproducing a message “exactly or approximately,” with noise and with decoding that can exploit statistical structure and the destination’s needs. citeturn30search3

Cognitive science makes reconstruction (not faithful replay) the default: Bartlett’s classic account treats remembering as “mental reconstructions” shaped by cultural attitudes and personal habits, not literal reproduction. citeturn31search13 Modern work on constructive memory likewise emphasizes that remembering is “constructive rather than reproductive,” and that flexibility (recombining elements) is adaptive. citeturn31search12 In the organizational literature you flagged, *some* authors get close to this reconstructive logic—but most stop short of an explicit, information-theoretic “lossy channel → inferential reconstruction → novelty” account.

## Closest matches in the organizational literature you listed

### Koçak & Puranam’s “Power of Babel” is the closest to an explicit lossy-communication model

Koçak & Puranam (2024) are the most explicit in modeling miscommunication *as* potentially productive. They propose a simple communication model: a sender wants to convey a “stimulus” (any object/event/attribute) and uses a “label” sent via some “technology (channel),” and the receiver infers what stimulus was intended; a “communication gap” (misunderstanding) exists when the inferred stimulus differs from the sender’s intended stimulus. citeturn32view2turn35view0

Crucially for your question, they locate misunderstandings in **systematic information loss/degeneration** arising from:
- **Differences in codes** (different mappings among stimuli/categories/labels), and
- **Fuzziness in codes** (polysemy/homonyms—one label referring to multiple stimuli), which creates ambiguity and allows different receivers to draw different associations. citeturn32view1turn32view2turn35view0  
They even note a formalizable representation of code differences (matrix mapping), which is unusually “information-structure”-friendly for organization/strategy scholarship. citeturn32view2turn35view0

Their central contribution is a typology of **three mechanisms** by which misunderstandings can yield positive outcomes—each tied to boundary conditions:
- **Truce:** communication gaps let parties believe they agree while preserving divergent beliefs; it requires decoupling (low interdependence) so the gap isn’t exposed by coordination breakdown. citeturn13view0turn12view2  
- **Search:** differing interpretations generate exploration; beneficial outcomes depend on a *rugged payoff landscape* (there are genuinely different local optima). citeturn13view0turn14view2  
- **Resonance:** the message aligns with pre-existing cognitive orientations/values, creating perceived understanding and motivational “buy-in” even when meaning transmission fails. citeturn13view1turn14view3turn12view1

The “search” and “resonance” mechanisms are the closest to your reconstructive-priors story. In “search,” they explicitly discuss mistranslation/context stripping: when an idea arrives “simplified” or decontextualized, receivers may adopt interpretations that become generative of new action paths. citeturn14view1turn14view2 In “resonance,” they explicitly posit that misunderstanding can work because receivers “tap into pre-existing meanings,” i.e., priors/schemas, to supply what the message does not specify. citeturn14view3turn13view1

Where they stop: they do **not** develop a full information-theoretic (Shannon-style) account with quantifiable loss (entropy, channel capacity, distortion metrics) or an explicit inferential decoding model (Bayesian priors → posterior reconstructions). They gesture toward pre-existing schemas/values and to channels/codes, but the cognitive reconstruction step is treated descriptively (and conditionally) rather than as the core generative engine. citeturn13view0turn14view3turn32view2

### Eisenberg’s strategic ambiguity anticipates “receiver-side reconstruction,” but not creativity as the outcome

Eisenberg (1984) is a foundational statement that ambiguity can be strategically functional in organizations, pushing against an “ideology of clarity.” He frames strategic ambiguity as a contingent communicative strategy used under multiple/conflicting goals, rather than a deviation from competence. citeturn11view0turn10search1

The most direct match to your mechanism is his interpersonal claim that receivers actively **complete** ambiguous messages: when people are “purposefully ambiguous,” audiences “fill in” what they believe is the appropriate context and meaning; more ambiguity yields more room for “projection.” citeturn11view0turn35view1 This is essentially “loss → reconstruction using priors,” even if he doesn’t use that language.

Where he stops: Eisenberg’s outcomes are mainly **organizational coordination and political/relational functionality** (unified diversity, deniability, flexibility, change facilitation), not a theory of how reconstruction yields genuinely novel idea synthesis. citeturn11view0turn35view1 The reconstructive move is there; the creativity mechanism is underdeveloped.

### Gioia, Nag & Corley extend strategic ambiguity into “novel sensemaking,” close to reconstruction-driven novelty

Gioia et al. (2012) argue that for major strategic change, an **ambiguous vision** can help initiate change because ambiguity permits multiple interpretations and reduces political resistance, while triggering members to rethink what the organization is and should become. citeturn34view0turn35view7

They explicitly describe ambiguity as a trigger for “novel sensemaking” that yields “localized interpretations and solutions,” and they emphasize that the vision must be interpreted “in light of existing conceptions”—a reconstruction-from-priors dynamic. citeturn34view0turn35view7 They also state that visionary ambiguity works partly because it allows employees to “apply their own interpretations” in ways they experience as consistent with the larger vision. citeturn35view7

Where they stop: their unit of analysis is change initiation and knowledge revision for alignment/adoption, not creativity per se; they do not formalize ambiguity as information loss nor model receiver reconstructions as generating *new* conceptual combinations beyond local solutions. citeturn35view7turn34view0

## Partial matches: brokering/translation theories that imply transformation, but not “lossy channel → priors → novel synthesis”

### Hargadon & Sutton’s technology brokering highlights transformation across boundaries, but not lossy reconstruction as the cause

Hargadon & Sutton (1997) explicitly note that “knowledge is imperfectly shared” across people, organizations, industries, and time; ideas can look “new and creative” as they “change form” and combine with other ideas for different users. citeturn29view1 That is adjacent to your thesis: “imperfection” in cross-boundary transfer is a condition of novelty.

They also argue that brokers do more than transfer; existing macro network accounts highlight resource flows but have not explicated the process by which information is transformed or combined, and they emphasize that solutions seldom arrive in “readily recognizable” or “easily adaptable” forms—necessitating internal routines for recognizing, storing, retrieving, and recombining ideas. citeturn29view1

Where they stop relative to your question: the creativity mechanism is **recombination via analogical retrieval and organizational memory**, not receiver-side reconstruction forced by information loss. Lossiness is acknowledged (“imperfectly shared,” “not readily recognizable”), but not theorized as the generative driver that *forces* inferential completion. citeturn29view1

### Burt’s structural holes: “translation advantage” and synthesis, but the arbitrage is about access—not about degradation

Burt (2004) is explicit that brokerage across structural holes gives access to alternatives and produces “new ideas” through “selection and synthesis” across groups. citeturn8view0turn35view3 He also explicitly includes a “translation” capability as part of brokerage: brokers have experience “translating information across groups” and doing “information arbitrage.” citeturn8view0turn35view3

He goes further by distinguishing levels of brokerage, culminating in synthesis: brokers can transfer best practice (translating into “language digestible” in the target group), draw analogies, and synthesize hybrid beliefs/behaviors. citeturn8view0

Where he stops: Burt is not arguing that **information loss** is generative—his story is that **social-structural separation creates nonredundant alternatives**, and brokers benefit by seeing and combining them. Translation is treated as competence for moving ideas across groups, not as lossy distortion that forces creative reconstruction. citeturn8view0turn35view3

### Perry-Smith & Mannucci’s “idea journey” focuses on networks across phases, not lossy transmission

Perry-Smith & Mannucci (2017) define four phases of the idea journey—generation, elaboration, championing, implementation—and argue that different network structures matter in each phase. citeturn5view2turn35view4 When they discuss championing, they explicitly invoke Burt’s notion that brokers have a “vision and translation advantage” for selling ideas to different audiences. citeturn28view0turn35view4

Where they stop: translation is framed as social-skill/position advantage in persuasion and legitimacy, not as information-theoretic loss that forces receivers to reconstruct meaning and thereby invent new syntheses. citeturn35view4turn28view0

## Clear non-matches: creativity theories centered on components, conflict, or dissent rather than lossy transmission

### Amabile’s componential theory doesn’t treat degraded communication as generative

In the componential theory, creativity is a novel and appropriate response to an open-ended task, driven by domain-relevant skills, creativity-relevant processes, and intrinsic task motivation, with the social environment as an external component shaping creativity. citeturn5view1turn43view0turn27view2 This is a foundational framework for organizational creativity, but it does not propose that creativity emerges because ideas are degraded in transit and reconstructed by receivers. Communication can be part of “environment,” but it isn’t theorized as a lossy-generative channel.

Where it stops: Amabile provides a general componential account of creative performance and environmental influences, but not a micro-theory of communicative distortion/reconstruction producing novelty. citeturn43view0turn27view2

### Leonard-Barton’s creative abrasion is about productive conflict among intact perspectives, not reconstruction from partial signals

Leonard & Straus’s “creative abrasion” argues that innovation depends on collisions among different ideas and cognitive approaches, and that managers must make the abrasion productive rather than personal. citeturn37view0turn43view1 They explicitly warn that homogenous groups filter ideas through similar “cognitive screens,” yielding familiar outputs. citeturn37view0turn43view1

Where they stop: the model treats misunderstanding as a coordination/relationship problem to manage; it does not treat information loss as a productive constraint that forces reconstruction into novel synthesis. citeturn37view0turn43view1

### Nemeth’s minority dissent research is a “cognitive stimulation by disagreement” mechanism, not lossy transmission

Nemeth’s work emphasizes that authentic dissent stimulates more open, divergent, and creative thinking—even when the dissenter is wrong—whereas majority views tend to promote convergent thinking. citeturn24view3turn35view8 In her group-creativity work, she argues that encouraging debate and criticism can outperform traditional brainstorming instructions that prohibit criticism. citeturn24view2turn23search3

Where it stops: dissent creates creativity through cognitive conflict and reconsideration, not through information being degraded in transmission and then reconstructed using priors. citeturn24view2turn35view8

## Who comes closest to an information-theoretic “lossy communication is generative” account

Koçak & Puranam (2024) are the closest match to what you’re looking for **within the organizational communication/strategy space** because they do all of the following in one place: (1) explicitly model communication as label transmission over a “channel,” (2) define misunderstandings as a systematic *gap* between intended and inferred stimulus, (3) locate causes in code differences and code fuzziness (structural sources of information loss/ambiguity), and (4) propose downstream generative mechanisms—especially search (exploration) and resonance (priors/schemas/values enabling completion). citeturn32view2turn13view0turn14view2turn14view3turn35view0

Eisenberg (1984) is the clearest antecedent for the **receiver-side reconstruction** idea (“fill in,” projection), but his aim is explaining how ambiguity helps organizing (unified diversity, deniability, flexibility), not how reconstruction yields novel synthesis. citeturn11view0turn35view1 Gioia et al. (2012) operationalize that same logic in strategic change: ambiguity triggers novel sensemaking and localized solutions by inviting members to reinterpret the vision through existing conceptions. citeturn35view7turn34view0

Gaver, Beaver & Benford (2003) are arguably the closest to your strongest claim (“loss forces reconstruction, reconstruction yields novelty”)—but they sit in HCI/design rather than core organizational creativity. They explicitly argue that ambiguity pushes people to interpret for themselves, supports multiple interpretations across backgrounds, and can turn technical limitations into virtues by letting users’ interpretations “supplement” the system. citeturn21view0turn16view1

By contrast, brokering/network theories (Burt; Hargadon & Sutton; Perry-Smith & Mannucci) get you *translation* and *transformation*, but mostly as a function of **access and recombination across domains**, not as a function of **information loss that forces receiver inference**. citeturn8view0turn29view1turn28view0

## Where the literature stops and what’s missing if you want a true information-theoretic account

Across the set you named, the dominant stopping points are consistent:

They rarely quantify or formalize **lossiness**. Even Koçak & Puranam—who mention channels/codes and even a matrix sketch—do not connect to information-theoretic quantities (entropy, distortion, capacity) or propose measurable “how lossy” constructs tied to creativity outcomes. citeturn32view2turn13view0turn35view0

They rarely treat the receiver as an explicit **decoder/inferential engine** with priors. Eisenberg’s projection claim and Koçak & Puranam’s resonance mechanism imply priors/schemas, and Gioia et al. describe interpreting visions in light of existing conceptions, but none of these become a computational or formal inference story (priors → posterior reconstructions → novelty). citeturn35view1turn14view3turn35view7

They often substitute “ambiguity helps coordination” for “ambiguity generates new content.” Strategic ambiguity (Eisenberg; Gioia et al.) primarily produces alignment, buy-in, flexibility, and conflict dampening; the novelty that appears is usually at the level of local interpretations/solutions during change, not theorized as a systematically new synthesis that *neither party contained* ex ante. citeturn35view1turn35view7

If you want to push this into a genuinely information-theoretic creativity account, the most “native” bridge is to treat organizational communication like Shannon’s problem (approximate reproduction under noise) combined with constructive memory (reconstruction and recombination). citeturn30search3turn31search12turn31search13 Koçak & Puranam already supply the organizational scaffolding (codes, fuzziness/differences, missing feedback, and outcome typology). citeturn32view1turn13view0turn35view0

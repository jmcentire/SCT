On the structural physics of high-intensity management
Frank Slootman's Amp It Up may be the most honest management book written in the past decade. Where most leadership literature trades in aspiration and abstraction, Slootman describes exactly what he does and why he believes it works. The method is simple in principle: raise standards until mediocrity becomes intolerable, align every incentive to a single objective, narrow focus until distraction becomes impossible, accelerate pace until delay becomes unacceptable, and shift strategy from defense to offense. He calls this amping it up. A more precise description would be variance compression—the systematic elimination of deviation in service of velocity.

The results demand attention. Data Domain sold for $2.4 billion. ServiceNow grew from infrastructure collapse to a market capitalization exceeding $160 billion. Snowflake executed the largest software initial public offering in history. Three companies, three transformations that would each define a career. Something in the method produces genuine value, and anyone who leads organizations should understand what that something is.

My interest in Slootman's work comes from a structural question that has occupied me for several years: why do organizations comprised of intelligent individuals converge on collective delusion? This question sits at the center of my book The Cage and the Mirror, which examines how the formal structures of organizations create constraints that leaders cannot escape through will or talent alone. Slootman's methodology offers a useful case study because he has made the compression explicit and intentional. Most leaders inherit variance-compressing structures without recognizing them as such; Slootman builds them on purpose and defends them in writing.

The disagreement
Slootman's diagnosis of organizational dysfunction is largely correct. Organizations do drift without energy injection. Process calcifies into ritual. Most companies tolerate too much, confuse motion with progress, and promote the visible over the valuable. On these points we are aligned. Merit matters more than category. Execution beats strategy. Standards should be high.

The disagreement concerns what high-intensity selection actually selects for. Slootman's claim is that it filters out low performers—the passengers, in his terminology—and concentrates excellence. My claim is that it filters out low performers, high performers with options who won't tolerate the conditions, and high performers who value dimensions of life the intensity excludes. What remains is a mixture of genuine excellence and mere endurance. If Slootman is right, his method produces concentrated capability. If I am right, his method produces the appearance of concentrated capability in an environment that has systematically eliminated the signals that would reveal the difference. The remainder of this essay examines the evidence on both sides.

How information disappears
The employees in Slootman's organizations face a specific incentive structure: their continued employment, their equity vesting, their career trajectory, and their daily experience all depend on being perceived as drivers rather than passengers. This creates what game theorists call preference divergence—the gap between what an agent wants and what a principal wants them to want.

Crawford and Sobel's foundational work on strategic communication demonstrates what happens as this gap increases: communication precision degrades through progressively coarser partitions. When sender and receiver interests align closely, detailed information flows freely. As the gap widens, senders shade their communications toward outcomes that serve their interests, receivers discount accordingly, and the information content of messages declines. At sufficient divergence, communication reaches what they call babbling equilibrium—messages become statistically independent of the underlying reality they purport to describe.

The 2020 CNBC investigation documented this dynamic at Snowflake with unusual clarity. Slootman himself acknowledged that after arriving, active employees refused to speak out about problems, terrified of their leaders turning against them, while sitting on valuable restricted stock they would not jeopardize. He had to pursue former employees—who had nothing to lose—to discover what was actually happening inside the organization he led. The selection environment had produced an organization where the CEO could not learn the truth from the people paid to tell it to him.

Who actually leaves
A 2025 study published in INFORMS by Cornell researchers examined a global pharmaceutical company using forced distribution systems and found that high performers who received unfairly downgraded ratings exhibited thirty-four to two hundred six percent higher voluntary turnover rates than peers receiving aligned ratings. The researchers tested retention interventions including higher bonuses, explicit fairness assurances, and manager coaching. None reversed the effect. High performers left anyway because they had options and refused to tolerate perceived injustice.

This aligns with earlier research by Trevor, Gerhart, and Boudreau, who studied 5,143 employees and discovered a curvilinear relationship between performance and turnover: both high performers and low performers show higher turnover than average performers. Low performers leave because they are pushed out or anticipate being pushed out. High performers leave because they have market mobility and will not accept conditions that average performers must tolerate.

Dutch longitudinal research using lottery-choice experiments found that risk-averse workers are less likely to move to other jobs, with the effect driven by job acceptance decisions rather than search effort—risk-averse people search but decline offers. Jiang and colleagues' meta-analysis of sixty-five samples found that job embeddedness predicts retention even after controlling for job satisfaction, commitment, and perceived alternatives, with the dimension of sacrifice—what employees would forfeit by leaving, including unvested equity—showing the highest negative correlation to turnover intention. In an organization like Snowflake, where equity compensation is substantial and backloaded, this dimension selects powerfully for workers who cannot afford to leave regardless of how they are experiencing the work.

What grit actually predicts
Credé and colleagues' meta-analysis of eighty-eight independent samples totaling over sixty-six thousand individuals found that grit—defined as perseverance and passion for long-term goals—is only moderately correlated with job performance and adds little predictive power beyond conscientiousness. The researcher noted that the questions used to measure grit are often nearly identical to questions used to measure conscientiousness, suggesting grit may be a repackaging of an existing construct. Other predictors like study habits and adjustment were far more strongly related to performance.

A 2025 meta-analysis of workplace resilience across nearly three hundred samples and over four hundred thousand individuals found that resilience exhibited positive but moderate associations with performance outcomes—and much stronger associations with psychological well-being and job attitudes. Resilience predicts sustainability of effort more than quality of output. The Keystone Partners research identified what they call the High-Performer Burnout Zone—employees with high performance but low resilience who are five times more likely to be actively job searching—and noted that the traits that make someone excel also make them least likely to notice they are running on empty until the damage is done.

The homogeneity problem
The Attraction-Selection-Attrition model explains how workforce composition evolves: organizations attract people who fit, select those who fit better, and attrit those who do not fit. Slootman's method accelerates this process dramatically, compressing the distribution of remaining employees toward a narrower band of characteristics in less time than normal organizational evolution would require.

Research on homogeneous teams shows that value congruence fosters certain individual and team benefits but can simultaneously diminish innovativeness and creativity in group outputs. The team that agrees efficiently may agree on the wrong things. Microsoft's lost decade under stack ranking provides a natural experiment: the selection pressure created internal competition that displaced external focus, caused top engineers to avoid working with each other, and drove employees toward quick wins that boosted rankings rather than long-term projects. The company missed the mobile and cloud transitions that defined the next era of computing while its internal metrics looked healthy.

The evidence for intensity
Slootman's results are facts that require explanation. ServiceNow was ninety days from failure when he arrived, according to Sequoia's Doug Leone. The platform was unstable, the infrastructure inadequate, and the company was being buried under its own success. He fixed it. The methods were intense, the turnover was high, and the outcome was a platform capable of supporting $160 billion in market value. Snowflake achieved the largest software IPO in history, 158 percent net revenue retention, and NPS scores in the low seventies. CFO Mike Scarpelli followed Slootman for eighteen years across all three companies. Warren Buffett made Snowflake his first U.S. tech IPO investment in sixty-four years.

One possible reconciliation is that variance compression is optimal for turnarounds and existential sprints. When the company is ninety days from death, the peacetime costs of exploration loss may not matter because there is no peacetime to protect. Narrow focus and relentless execution may be exactly right when the alternative is extinction.

Another possibility is that Slootman's personal judgment allows him to mitigate costs that would sink other leaders attempting the same approach. His calibration system requires him to personally evaluate employees across the organization, which means his individual capacity to identify genuine excellence operates as a check on the statistical tendencies of the selection mechanism. The method may work for him specifically, in ways that would not transfer to imitators who lack his discernment.

A third possibility is that the costs are real but deferred—that Slootman's method extracts value in ways that create debts his successors must service. ServiceNow grew sevenfold after he left while simultaneously becoming a Best Places to Work company under Bill McDermott. The 2024 Snowflake breach occurred under his watch; the FTC investigation and consolidated lawsuits followed his departure. These observations do not prove causation, but they raise questions about whether the organizations he built were optimized for metrics visible during his tenure at the expense of metrics that became visible later.

A physics problem
The framing I want to resist is the one that treats this disagreement as moral. Both positions are empirical claims about what selection mechanisms actually select for, and the evidence should settle it eventually—though the evidence is difficult to gather because the selection environments that would generate it also suppress the signals we would need to evaluate them.

The concept I have been developing under the name dysmemic pressure treats organizational dysfunction as a physics problem. When preference divergence between organizational agents reaches sufficient magnitude, communication becomes statistically independent of reality while transmission biases ensure that dysfunctional signals outcompete accurate ones in the organizational meme pool. The leader who creates intense pressure on employees to be perceived as drivers is creating a selection environment whose physics produce dysfunction as equilibrium output, regardless of the leader's intentions.

This reframing explains why standard interventions so often fail. Culture change initiatives, leadership development programs, and values alignment exercises treat equilibrium outcomes as behavioral problems amenable to inspiration or training. But if the dysfunction emerges from the selection environment rather than from individual choices within it, changing the behavior of individuals cannot solve the problem—new individuals will be selected by the same environment into the same patterns. The intervention that might work is architectural: changing the selection mechanism itself so that different signals outcompete the dysfunctional ones.

The question that remains
I do not know whether Slootman is right or I am right, and I am suspicious of anyone who claims certainty on questions this complex. The research on turnover and forced ranking does not support the simple story that intensity concentrates excellence. Against this stands Slootman's track record—three companies, three transformations, results that speak for themselves. Perhaps he has found something the research has not captured. Perhaps his personal judgment compensates for statistical tendencies that would doom imitators. Perhaps the conditions he operates in—turnarounds, hypergrowth, existential stakes—are genuinely different from the conditions the research examines.

What I am confident about is the frame. This is a question of mechanism—what variance compression actually produces, what selection environments actually select for, and whether the organizations that result are as capable as they appear or whether they have traded capacity for the appearance of capacity in ways that become visible only when the environment shifts. The answer matters beyond Slootman because his methods are being copied by leaders who lack his results and may lack his judgment, in organizations that may not survive the costs his organizations have been able to absorb.

The cage that most leaders build is invisible to them. Slootman has made his visible, defended it in writing, and produced results that demand engagement. Whether the cage contains the excellence he claims or merely its appearance under conditions optimized to prevent anyone from checking—that is the question I cannot yet answer, and I suspect he cannot either. The selection environment he has created ensures that the people who might know are precisely the people who cannot say.

Most CEOs, when sued by the SEC, compress toward defensive hedging. Legal tells them to say nothing quotable. Investor relations sanitizes the earnings call. Brian Armstrong did the opposite: his hedge-to-assertion ratio dropped 73% under regulatory assault, and his lexical diversity expanded 17%. Every metric moved opposite to institutional compression.

This analysis examines Coinbase quarterly earnings calls spanning November 2022 through May 2025, extracting only Armstrong's speaking portions. The corpus totals 5,505 words across three phases: pre-litigation baseline (Q3 2022 through Q1 2023, 1,920 words), active litigation following the SEC's June 2023 suit (Q2 2023 through Q1 2024, 1,942 words), and post-dismissal victory (Q2 2024 through Q1 2025, 1,643 words). Primary metrics are lexical diversity (unique tokens divided by total), Shannon entropy (base-2 logarithm), and hedge-to-assertion ratio.

The pre-litigation baseline shows conventional CEO hedging. Lexical diversity registered 0.304; Shannon entropy 7.92 bits; hedge-to-assertion ratio 0.82. Armstrong spoke of regulatory progress with measured optimism: "bipartisan support," "moving in a positive direction," "regulatory clarity is one of the things that could help." The hedging was textbook. "We can't actually predict the future. I don't want to make the lawyers too nervous here on forward-looking statements." Hedge density ran 14.1 instances per thousand words. This is the linguistic signature of a public company CEO who understands the rules.

The SEC lawsuit inverted the pattern. During active litigation, hedge-to-assertion ratio collapsed to 0.25—a 69% decline toward assertive language. Hedge density fell to 3.1 per thousand. Lexical diversity rose to 0.320. The vocabulary shifted from compliance to confrontation: "regulation by enforcement," "a few outliers in our government painting outside the bounds of the law," "we will not allow American leadership here to be destroyed." The phrase "52 million Americans" appeared repeatedly as mobilization rhetoric. Standard disclaimers gave way to declarations.

The post-dismissal period completed the trajectory. Lexical diversity expanded to 0.355—a 17% increase from baseline, the inverse of typical CEO compression. Hedge-to-assertion ratio bottomed at 0.22, down 73% overall. Shannon entropy rose to 8.05 bits. Armstrong's Q4 2024 call opened: "Crypto's voice was heard loud and clear." "It's the dawn of a new era." "It's been incredibly validating." By Q1 2025, after the dismissal: "a major judicial win," "we're entering a golden age for crypto." Assertion density peaked at 21.9 per thousand words, up 27% from baseline. The vocabulary had rotated from defensive compliance through adversarial mobilization to triumphalism.

Armstrong controls approximately 64% of Coinbase's voting power through Class B shares carrying 20:1 super-voting rights. His economic ownership is roughly 15%—he bears a fraction of downside risk while controlling all strategic decisions. The standard fiduciary calculation (balance litigation risk against settlement cost, say nothing quotable) does not apply when shareholders cannot remove you for choosing the aggressive path.

The Cage predicts that fiduciary pressure compresses executive language toward defensive mean. Armstrong confirms the mechanism by showing what happens when you remove it: language expands toward variance the executive actually prefers. Structural protection enables combative variance. The architecture determines the output.

The prediction follows from the physics: Armstrong's assertive vocabulary persists until governance changes. If voting control ever dilutes below majority through share sales, acquisitions paid in stock, or class conversion, watch the earnings calls first. "Dawn of a new era" becomes "we remain focused on execution." "Golden age" becomes "constructive dialogue with regulators." The vocabulary will compress because the filtering architecture finally gets installed.

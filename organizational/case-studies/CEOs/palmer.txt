Palmer Luckey sold Oculus to Facebook for two billion dollars, then got fired for donating nine thousand to a Trump meme group. His first post-departure interview was at a Japanese anime convention, discussing VR's future with journalists who found him on cosplay forums. The question is whether the linguistic data reflects what happened when he stopped pretending to be anyone other than himself.

This analysis examines Luckey's interview transcripts across three governance contexts: the Oculus period (2012-2017), including time as a Facebook employee; the transition period (2017-2019), after his firing and during Anduril's founding; and the Anduril period (2020-2025), as founder of a private defense company. The corpus totals 7,161 words drawn from technology conferences, podcast interviews, and television appearances. Primary metrics are lexical diversity, Shannon entropy, hedge-to-assertion ratio, and domain-vocabulary density tracking VR, defense, and political terminology.

The Oculus period establishes baseline under public-company constraints: lexical diversity at 0.262, hedge-to-assertion ratio at 0.457, VR vocabulary at 21.0 instances per thousand words, defense and political vocabulary essentially absent (0.37 each). Luckey hedged considerably during this period, deploying "I think," "maybe," "time will tell," and "I don't know" at rates that suggest careful navigation of corporate communications expectations.

The transition period shows peak uncertainty: lexical diversity rose to 0.285, but hedge-to-assertion ratio spiked to 0.636, the highest across all periods. A fired founder finding his footing speaks tentatively. Political vocabulary emerged (4.45 per thousand) as Luckey began articulating his identity outside corporate constraints: "fiscally conservative, pro-freedom, little-L libertarian, and big-R Republican."

The Anduril period shows full liberation. Lexical diversity climbed to 0.303, a 15.6% increase from the Oculus period. Hedge-to-assertion ratio dropped to 0.364, the lowest across all periods. The domain vocabulary inverted entirely: VR terms collapsed 68% (from 21.0 to 6.8 per thousand), defense vocabulary exploded from 0.37 to 14.53 per thousand, political vocabulary rose from 0.37 to 8.62 per thousand. The man who once carefully discussed "field of view" and "price points" now deploys "traitor to the nation," "squirt guns or slingshots," and "we are screwed" without apparent concern for stakeholder sensitivities.

The governance structure explains the trajectory. At Facebook, Luckey was an employee of a public company subject to corporate communications review; he explicitly noted he "had to restrain" himself and "could not cosplay while working at Facebook." At Anduril, he controls a private company with no public shareholders to appease. The earnings call does not exist. The PR handler has no veto. The Cage did not fail at Facebook; it functioned exactly as designed. Resistance was punished. Compliance was eventually punished too, but for reasons outside the communications structure.

The pattern inverts the standard compression narrative. Where most executives show vocabulary narrowing over time as institutional gravity pulls toward the mean, Luckey's vocabulary expanded when he escaped public-company constraints. His hedge-to-assertion ratio dropped 20% from Oculus to Anduril; his willingness to deploy politically charged and confrontational language increased by an order of magnitude. Whether that expanded vocabulary reflects wisdom or recklessness is a separate question; the structural point is that the Cage's removal permitted the expansion either way.

The prediction: watch Anduril's eventual IPO. If the company goes public, Luckey's linguistic variance should compress toward the patterns we observe in other public-company executives. The data from this analysis provides a baseline against which to measure that compression. For now, he remains the rare case study of what a founder sounds like when the Cage opens from the inside.
